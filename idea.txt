===============================================================================
 KALACHAKRA — the whole idea, in plain words
===============================================================================

Read this first. No jargon, no setup — just what this thing actually is, why it
exists, and what happens when YOU run it. If you finish this page you'll "get"
the project. The step-by-step commands live in instructions.txt; this file is the
mental picture that makes those commands make sense.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 1. What is this, really?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Think of a weather radar. Not for rain — for the *geometry of the solar system*.

Every moment, the Sun, Moon, and planets sit at particular angles to each other
and to any spot on Earth. Those angles form patterns: sometimes things line up
(tension, concentration), sometimes they spread out (release), sometimes a rare
configuration shows up that hasn't happened in thousands of years. Kalachakra
measures all of that, turns it into numbers, and shows it to you as a living,
glowing map of Earth.

That's the key word: MEASURES. This is not astrology. There's no "you'll meet a
tall stranger" text anywhere in it. It refuses to interpret. It only reports cold,
objective geometry — "these two bodies are 0.04 degrees apart," "this pattern is
in the rarest 0.1% of the last ten thousand years," "the energy here is rising."
What that *means* is entirely up to you. The project's whole personality is: give
honest math, add zero folklore.

So in one sentence: **Kalachakra is a cosmic weather station that watches the
shape of the solar system over 10,256 years and paints it onto a globe as pure
geometry.**


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 2. Why 10,256 years? And why is that a big deal?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The timeline runs from 3102 BCE (an ancient reference point called the Kali Yuga
epoch) all the way to 7154 CE. That's 10,256 years.

The clock doesn't tick in hours. It ticks every 24 seconds (an old unit called a
"Vighatika"). Why so fine? Because in 24 seconds the sky rotates just enough
(~0.1 degree) to matter for the horizon. Tick every 24 seconds for 10,256 years
and you get about **13.4 BILLION snapshots** of the solar system. Each snapshot
is the exact position and speed of 10 bodies.

That's the "big" version. You do NOT need to compute all 13.4 billion to use the
project — more on that in a second. But that's the full ambition: a complete,
frame-by-frame movie of the solar system's geometry across ten millennia.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 3. What actually happens, start to finish (the pipeline)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Here's the whole machine, in order. Picture it as a factory line:

  (1) ASK THE SKY.  A real astronomy engine (the Swiss Ephemeris — the same kind
      of precise planetary calculator astronomers use) tells us exactly where
      every body is at a given instant. Real positions, not made up. This is the
      raw ingredient.

  (2) PROJECT IT ONTO EARTH.  Those positions are "global" — true for the whole
      planet. We then work out how the sky looks from 122,880 evenly-spread
      points across the Earth's surface (a geodesic mesh — imagine a soccer-ball
      grid wrapped over the globe). Now every spot on Earth has its own local
      picture of the sky.

  (3) SQUEEZE OUT THE PATTERN.  A neural network (a "spherical autoencoder" plus
      a "Fourier neural operator" — fancy names, simple job) learns to compress
      that giant field of numbers down to a tiny fingerprint of just 64 numbers
      that still captures the essential shape of the moment. It teaches itself,
      unsupervised — nobody labels anything. It just finds the structure.

  (4) TURN IT INTO TOKENS + A RARITY SCORE.  Those fingerprints get sorted into a
      vocabulary of 4,096 recurring "shapes" (like an alphabet of cosmic moments),
      and each moment gets a RARITY score: how unusual is this, compared to all
      ten thousand years? Common = boring. Rare = something worth looking at.

  (5) FILE IT SO IT'S INSTANT TO SEARCH.  All of that gets stored in a smart,
      layered database. You can ask "show me the rarest events over India in
      January 2024" and get an answer in milliseconds, even against a massive
      archive. It keeps summaries at three zoom levels (second-by-second, hourly,
      daily) so zooming out over centuries stays fast.

  (6) SHOW IT TO YOU.  Finally, the numbers become something you can SEE and feel:
      a glowing globe. And here's the clever part — the visuals aren't decoration,
      they're an exact translation (see section 5).

Steps 1-2 are pure, verified astronomy. Steps 3-4 are the "AI" part. Steps 5-6
are storage and the pretty, useful front end.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 4. Two ways to run it: the easy way and the epic way
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This matters, because it decides how much time and disk you need.

  EASY WAY (start here — minutes, on a normal laptop, no downloads):
    The astronomy engine has a built-in mode that needs zero data files and
    covers roughly 3000 BCE to 3000 CE — i.e. all of recorded history and today.
    You can immediately get real readings, scan a year for real eclipses, train a
    small model, build an index, and open every visual. This is the "see it work
    today" path. Most people live here.

  EPIC WAY (the full 10,256 years — serious hardware):
    To reach 3102 BCE and the far future you download a long-range planetary data
    set (a ~40 MB set of files — one command does it and configures everything).
    Generating the entire 13.4-billion-frame movie is about 1.9 TB on disk as
    written (there's a clear path to shrink that severalfold with compression,
    not yet enabled), and training the model over the full cycle is a very long
    run on a powerful machine (an Apple M4 Max is the reference) — realistically
    the heaviest, least-tested part of the project. Same exact commands as the
    easy way — you just point them at the full data and let it run.

The beautiful bit: **you learn everything on the easy path, and the epic path is
the identical workflow scaled up.** Nothing is different except the size and the
wait.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 5. The visuals — and why they're not "just pretty"
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
There are two things you open in a web browser.

  A) THE GLOBE (index.html).
     A rotating Earth made of thousands of points. Each point glows based on the
     geometric "potential" and "shear" at that spot right now (or at whatever
     date you asked for). Bright, tense zones vs. calm zones, at a glance. Great
     for "what does the whole planet look like at this instant?"

  B) THE RADAR (radar.html) — this is the showpiece.
     This one uses an idea the project calls the "isomorphic transducer." Big
     phrase, cool concept: instead of arbitrarily picking colors, it translates
     each *kind* of math into the matching *kind* of physical sensation, exactly:

        - how strong a moment is        -> how bright it glows (like real light)
        - how RARE it is                -> its color temperature (rare = hot blue/
                                           white, like a hotter star; common = red)
        - the four "rhythms" of time     -> a real spread of visible-light colors
          (fast daily stuff vs slow
           planet-cycle stuff)
        - the flow/direction of change   -> a moving fluid, like wind on a weather
                                           map (things "applying" vs "separating")
        - the 64-number fingerprint      -> actual bumps and valleys in the globe's
                                           surface (mountains and trenches that ARE
                                           the math)

     Because every translation is exact and reversible, the optical state can be
     read back into the original 64 numbers with no loss (verified to machine
     precision in the tests). One honest nuance: the surface shape is a faithful,
     lossless *encoding* of those 64 numbers — a container your eyes can read —
     not a claim that "this mountain is caused by that planet." The math is
     preserved perfectly; the artistic-looking terrain is how it's stored, not a
     physical map of the sky.

     You can double-click any point to pop open a "Sidebar Inspector" that shows
     the raw numbers behind that spot: which bodies, their positions and speeds,
     the rarity, whether an eclipse is near, and so on. Shift-double-click zooms
     into a small region and computes it in fine detail on the fly.

Both open right in your browser from the project's own server — no fiddling.

(There's also a plain data feed — a REST API and a gRPC service — for people who
want to pull the numbers into their own tools instead of looking at the globe.)


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 6. So... what will *I* actually DO when I run it?
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Realistically, your first hour looks like this:

  1. Install it (one line).
  2. Type a command and instantly get a real reading — e.g. ask for the 2024
     total solar eclipse and watch it report the Sun and Moon sitting 0.04
     degrees apart. Real. Verifiable. Yours to check against the news.
  3. Scan a whole year and watch it FIND the real eclipses on its own, ranked by
     how unusual they are.
  4. Run one turn-key training command. It quietly makes its own practice data,
     trains the neural network, prints the "loss" dropping (that's it learning),
     and saves the trained model to a folder.
  5. Build the searchable index from that model.
  6. Start the server and open the globe and the radar in your browser. Spin the
     Earth. Double-click things. Watch it glow.

Later, if you want the real thing at full scale, you run ONE setup command to
pull the long-range data, then kick off the big generation + training and let it
churn for as long as it takes. The commands are the same ones you already learned.

That's it. You go from "typed a command" to "holding a living map of the solar
system's geometry across ten thousand years" — and every number in it is honest.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 7. A few honest promises (so you know what you're getting)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  * It's REAL, not a demo. The astronomy is genuine and checked against real
    eclipses and conjunctions. The model genuinely trains. The database genuinely
    answers in milliseconds. The picture genuinely reverses back into the math.
  * It makes NO predictions and tells NO fortunes. It reports geometry. Meaning
    is yours to add or not.
  * The easy path runs today on a laptop. The full 10,256-year path is a real,
    heavy compute job — terabytes of data and a very long train that is the
    least-tested part of the system — and the project is upfront about that
    instead of pretending it's instant. Two honest caveats about the deep past:
    (a) how far back Earth's exact rotation is known is uncertain by an hour or
    two around 3102 BCE, so the *where-on-Earth* part of very old maps is fuzzy
    even though the sky geometry stays precise; (b) the full-scale index only
    keeps the statistically rare moments in fine detail (that's the whole point
    of the rarity score) with coarse daily summaries everywhere else.
  * You can verify it yourself: it ships with 150+ automated tests, and every
    quick-scale command here has been run for real. The full-scale run is a
    scaling exercise on your own hardware, not something that fits in a test.
